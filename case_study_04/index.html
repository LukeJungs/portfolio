Context

V-TAG launched in 2020 as a Canadian vaping brand operating under the Tobacco and Vaping Products Act (TVPA), the federal framework administered by Health Canada. From the outset, the design challenge was not simply aesthetic — every surface of every package was a regulated document. Warning language, nicotine concentration, ingredient disclosure, bilingual parity, age restriction symbols, and UPC barcode standards were all mandatory, non-negotiable, and subject to revision as federal guidance evolved.

The brand required a complete visual identity built from the ground up: logo, colour system, typographic standards, and a packaging architecture capable of scaling across multiple product lines and dozens of flavour variants — all while maintaining full regulatory compliance on every printed unit shipped to Canadian retailers.

"Every centimetre of the package was both a brand decision and a legal obligation."

The team was small and cross-functional. Chuck led product design, Baiwen handled photography, Brian Chang managed operations and CEO oversight, and DHK in Korea produced high-resolution product renders and video assets. As the in-house graphic designer alongside Naeun, the scope covered everything printed or displayed — from the V-TAG wordmark to the Shopify product detail page, from the internal policy documents to the physical dieline submitted to the manufacturing facility in Shenzhen.

Analysis — Regulatory & Design Constraints

Before any visual work began, a thorough analysis of the regulatory environment was essential. Health Canada's Vaping Products Labelling and Packaging Regulations defined the minimum requirements: mandatory bilingual warning statements in both English and French, nicotine concentration displayed in mg/mL, a standardized AVERTISSEMENT block with prescribed minimum font sizes, and a poison warning with the skull-and-crossbones symbol for liquid nicotine products.

Beyond federal mandates, provincial age restrictions added further complexity. Certain provinces — including Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Quebec, Northwest Territories, Nunavut, and Yukon — required explicit 18+ or 19+ labelling on the physical package. Each symbol had to be sized, positioned, and reproduced at sufficient resolution for print production.

The bilingual requirement was the most persistent design constraint. French translations of flavour names, warning statements, and ingredient lists consistently ran longer than their English equivalents — Framboise Bleue versus Blue Razz, Avertissement versus Warning. Every layout decision had to accommodate both languages simultaneously, at equal visual weight, within the same spatial envelope. This was not a translation problem. It was a typographic systems problem.

Design — Brand Identity & Logo System

The V-TAG wordmark was designed to communicate precision and modernity — a clean sans-serif logotype paired with a custom chevron symbol derived from the letterform of the "V". The mark needed to function at the scale of a full-bleed package front and at the reduced size of a side panel printed at 6pt. Scalability and legibility across production sizes were primary criteria, not secondary considerations.

The identity system was governed by a formal design brief — a structured document specifying logo construction grids, primary colour values (Black #121212 / White #FFFFFF), and use-case mapping across every application surface. Different logo lockups were defined for distinct contexts: V00 for official documents and legally related packaging, V01 for marketing, social media, and advertisement, V02 for banners and wall installation. This governance ensured the mark remained consistent across regulated materials, commercial collateral, and digital touchpoints — even as the team and production volume scaled.

Design — Compliance Architecture

The most technically demanding aspect of the packaging system was the warning block architecture. Health Canada mandates that the WARNING / AVERTISSEMENT statement occupy a clearly delineated zone on the primary display panel, with both languages present at equal visual prominence. On a narrow side panel — such as the EON single-device box — this meant compressing a multi-line bilingual warning into a vertical column less than 25mm wide, while maintaining the prescribed minimum type size.

The solution was a modular warning block system: a fixed-width bordered rectangle containing the English warning in bold, followed immediately by the French equivalent, with a dividing rule between them. This block was treated as an immovable design element — every other layout decision on the panel was made around it, not before it. Typography, product imagery, and brand elements were positioned in the remaining space after compliance requirements were satisfied.

"The warning block was not a constraint on the design. It was the foundation the design was built around."

The bilingual challenge extended beyond the warning block. Flavour names required equal-weight bilingual treatment on the front panel — Blueberry | Myrtille, Mango | Mangue, Mint | Menthe — with the vertical bar acting as both a visual separator and a typographic rhythm element. The French names were never subordinated to the English; they shared the same typeface weight, size, and colour. This was both a regulatory requirement and a design principle.

Design — Colour System

The colour system was built on Pantone Uncoated (U) references to ensure consistency across the manufacturing chain. Each of the eleven EON flavour variants was assigned a primary Pantone colour — Mint (3536U), Blue Lemon (293U + 121U), Mango (121U), Blue Razz (299U), Strawberry Banana (2347U + 121U), Strawberry Kiwi (2293U), Blueberry (7684U), Lush Watermelon (362U), Peach (176U), Classic (2035U), Lychee (White) — and a corresponding secondary tone for the device ring and accent elements. Every colour decision was documented in a specification sheet submitted alongside the production file to the factory.

Development — Production & Manufacturing Handoff

The packaging system spanned three distinct product lines, each with its own structural requirements. The ENVOI Starter Kit was a rigid sleeve box containing the device, pod, charger, and manual — requiring a full dieline with window cut and a four-panel compliance layout. The ENVOI Device Kit and Pod Kit followed a modular architecture — the same structural template adapted for three colourways (black, red, royal blue) and multiple nicotine concentrations (2%, 3%). The EON single-device disposable extended this system across eleven flavour variants, each requiring a unique front panel colour while maintaining identical compliance architecture across every SKU.

Production files were prepared for a manufacturing facility in Shenzhen, China. This required converting all Pantone references to CMYK process values verified against the supplier's press profile, specifying paper stock weight and finish for each package component, and confirming barcode quiet zones met GS1 minimum standards for retail scanning. Every file submitted to the factory was accompanied by a written specification sheet — paper weight, lamination type, die-cut registration marks, and colour approval reference.

Development — Brand Extension & Expo Collateral

The V-TAG identity was designed to scale beyond the product package. As the brand prepared for trade and consumer expo appearances, the same visual language — the V chevron, the stark black-and-white primary palette, the typographic precision — was extended into promotional and event materials. The Expo Bag brief is a direct record of this process: a press-ready design document specifying material grade (Max 200 gsm C1S premium art-board), handle specification (2 × 45 cm black PP rope), print colour (1 colour black), production quantity (1,500 units), and unit cost (0.89 EUR).

The document itself is evidence of production literacy — the ability to translate a brand decision into a vendor-ready specification. This is the same discipline applied to every packaging dieline: not just designing the visual, but owning the full production chain from concept to factory handoff.

Implementation — Digital & E-Commerce

The packaging language did not stop at the printed box. The V-TAG website and Shopify storefront extended the same visual system — high-contrast product photography, minimal typographic hierarchy, and the V-TAG wordmark as the primary brand signal — into the digital commerce environment. Product detail pages (PDPs) were designed to carry the same information architecture as the physical package: flavour identity, nicotine strength, product contents, and compliance language, translated into a scrollable retail interface.

The website design also served as the primary B2C touchpoint for Canadian consumers, complementing the retail distribution channel. Maintaining visual consistency between the physical package and the digital storefront was a deliberate brand decision — ensuring that a customer who encountered V-TAG on a retail shelf and then visited the website experienced the same identity, the same hierarchy, and the same tone.

ADDIE Model — Applied Framework

Craft, iteration, and the discipline of repetition

The packaging system was not designed once. It was designed, reviewed, corrected, translated, proofread, corrected again, submitted to the factory, revised against press proofs, and corrected once more. This iterative cycle — repeated across every SKU, every colourway, every language revision — is the defining characteristic of regulated goods design. The ADDIE model was not applied as a formal methodology; it describes, accurately, how the work actually proceeded.

Analysis preceded every design decision: regulatory requirements were mapped before layouts were opened. Design built the system — the warning block architecture, the bilingual flavour name treatment, the Pantone colour governance, the logo construction grid. Development translated that system into production-ready files: dielines, specification sheets, and vendor briefs for a factory in Shenzhen. Implementation was the print run — and the moment when every typographic decision was tested against physical reality. Evaluation was the correction cycle: catching a misplaced accent in dépendance, verifying that Framboise Bleue fit the same column width as Blue Razz, confirming that the barcode quiet zone had not been violated by a bleed mark.

Outcome

The V-TAG packaging system launched across Canadian retail in 2021 and remained in production through 2022. More than twenty individual SKUs were designed, produced, and distributed — each compliant with Health Canada's federal vaping regulations, each carrying bilingual content at equal visual weight, and each traceable back to a single, coherent design system.

The project demonstrated that regulatory compliance and brand coherence are not in opposition. The constraints imposed by Health Canada — mandatory warning blocks, bilingual parity, prescribed symbol sizes — became the structural logic of the packaging system. Working within those constraints produced a visual language that was disciplined, legible, and scalable: qualities that defined the brand as much as the Pantone colours or the V-TAG wordmark.

The iterative nature of the work — the repeated proofreading cycles, the translation checks, the factory revision rounds — built a production discipline that extended across every other design output at V-TAG: internal documents, the company website, B2B price sheets, the Shopify storefront, and expo collateral. The craft of getting it right, every time, on every surface, was the consistent standard.

This project is, in essence, a study in regulated advertising design. The review cycles, the compliance checkpoints, the bilingual accuracy requirements — these are structurally identical to the MLR/PRC review process in healthcare and pharmaceutical communications. The discipline of designing within strict regulatory frameworks, without sacrificing brand coherence, is transferable across any regulated industry.
